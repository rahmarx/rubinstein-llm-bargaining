# Splitting the Pie Under the Clock: LLM Negotiators Against the Rubinstein Subgame-Perfect Benchmark

Raissa Marques da Silva
CSCE 631 — Intelligent Agents: Computational Game Solving, Texas A&M University, Fall 2026

## Abstract

Large language models are increasingly deployed as autonomous negotiators, yet it is unclear how closely their behavior tracks what game theory says a rational negotiator should do. We test three LLMs (Claude Sonnet 4.5, GPT-5-mini, Gemini 2.5 Flash) against Rubinstein's (1982) subgame-perfect equilibrium (SPE) for alternating-offers bargaining, across seven game variants (finite-horizon, stochastic infinite-horizon, and asymmetric-discount-factor conditions) and two prompt conditions (equilibrium explained vs. not), with a scripted, exactly-rational opponent as a noise-free baseline (1,260 dialogues). Left to compute the equilibrium themselves, all three models deviate by 9-14 points on a 100-point pie; stating the equilibrium in the prompt closes most of this gap for every model, pointing to a "knowing" rather than "doing" failure. A deviation taxonomy further shows the three models fail differently — Claude toward fairness-driven compromise, GPT-5-mini toward exploitation beyond the equilibrium, Gemini toward outright negotiation breakdown. Two follow-up extensions probe this further: a predict-then-play design that elicits each model's stated equilibrium belief before it negotiates finds that the stated prediction is no more accurate than the model's own revealed play, complicating a simple "knows it but doesn't act on it" story; and an LLM-vs-LLM extension shows that auditing only against a rational scripted opponent understates risk, since pairing two imperfect models produces breakdown rates (up to 50% in one pairing) with no analogue in either model's individual performance against the rational baseline. We also document and correct a methodological artifact — a response-token cap too tight for two of the three models, silently inflating their measured breakdown rates — treating the discovery and fix as part of the study's contribution rather than a footnote. The principal limitation is that the Phase 1 main result characterizes behavior against a deterministic rational opponent; the smaller-sample Phase 2 extensions probe, but do not fully replace, a need for a larger-scale LLM-vs-LLM study.

## 1. Introduction

Large language models are increasingly deployed as autonomous negotiators: pricing agents that haggle over quotes, scheduling agents that trade meeting slots, procurement agents that split costs between vendors. Each of these is, formally, a bargaining problem — and bargaining problems have a mature game-theoretic answer. Rubinstein's (1982) alternating-offers model gives a unique, closed-form prediction for how two fully rational, impatient players should split a fixed surplus, derived entirely from the primitives of the interaction: who moves when, what a delay costs, and what happens if agreement is never reached. If an LLM is going to negotiate on a person's behalf, it is natural to ask how its behavior compares to this benchmark — not because real negotiations should always converge to it (human bargaining is full of norms, fairness concerns, and incomplete information that the classical model abstracts away), but because the benchmark gives a *precise, falsifiable* yardstick: a model that systematically leaves money on the table, or one that over-claims past what a rational opponent would tolerate, has a measurable and economically meaningful flaw.

**Research question.** How far do LLM negotiators deviate from the subgame-perfect equilibrium (SPE) of the Rubinstein bargaining game when playing against an opponent who plays exactly that equilibrium, and what explains the gap — a failure to compute the equilibrium, or a failure to act on it once computed?

I test this with three LLMs (Claude Sonnet 4.5, GPT-5-mini, and Gemini 2.5 Flash) across seven variants of the alternating-offers game spanning finite deadlines, infinite-horizon stochastic discounting, and asymmetric patience, under two prompting conditions — the equilibrium share stated explicitly, or left for the model to work out. A scripted opponent that always proposes and accepts at its exact SPE value serves as a noise-free rational baseline, so that any measured deviation is attributable to the LLM rather than to randomness on both sides of the table.

**Contributions.** (1) A pre-registered, confidence-interval-backed benchmark of three LLM families against the exact Rubinstein SPE across seven game variants and two prompt conditions (1,260 dialogues total), with a *deviation taxonomy* that classifies not just how far a model strays from equilibrium but in which direction and why. (2) A predict-then-play extension that elicits each model's stated belief about the equilibrium *before* it negotiates, directly separating "does not know the equilibrium" from "knows it but does not act on it." (3) An LLM-vs-LLM extension (Section 4.5), both within a model family and across families, testing whether the scripted-opponent result generalizes to a setting where the opponent is itself a reasoning (and occasionally irrational) agent rather than a deterministic rational one. (4) A documented, fixed methodological artifact — response-token truncation that was silently inflating two models' measured breakdown rates — reported here because distinguishing a real finding from a measurement artifact is itself part of doing this kind of study honestly.

**Scope and how it evolved.** The original proposal considered crossing all three discount factors with all three finite-horizon deadlines (a 3×3 design); after the professor's feedback on the proposal raised the importance of a fixed, pre-registered sample size and call budget, the finite-horizon sweep was reduced to a single shared discount factor (δ = 0.8, the midpoint of the infinite-horizon grid) crossed with three deadlines, keeping the core grid at seven variants so that N = 30 dialogues per cell was affordable within the course's API budget. The progress report described two further extensions — LLM-vs-LLM play and the predict-then-play elicitation — as designed but not yet executed; both are completed and reported here, at a reduced sample size (N = 10/cell) appropriate to their role as targeted follow-up probes rather than a second full-powered study.

## 2. Related Work

**Classical bargaining theory.** The benchmark this project tests against is Rubinstein's (1982) alternating-offers model: two players take turns proposing a split of a fixed pie, and the unique subgame-perfect equilibrium is derived by backward induction from the primitive that delay is costly. Binmore, Rubinstein, and Wolinsky (1986) establish the equivalence between a fixed per-period discount factor and a constant per-period probability that bargaining breaks down — the "risk-of-breakdown" interpretation this project uses to implement the infinite-horizon condition as a finite stochastic process. These papers supply the closed-form predictions (Section 3) against which every dialogue in this study is scored; nothing here extends the theory itself.

**Empirical game-theoretic analysis.** Wellman's (2006) methodological framing, developed further in Wellman, Tuyls, and Greenwald's (2025) survey, treats strategic behavior as something to be *measured* empirically from simulated or observed play rather than assumed from a game's declarative description — exactly the stance this project takes toward LLMs as empirical rather than axiomatic agents. The present study is narrower than full empirical game-theoretic analysis (EGTA): rather than sweeping a strategy-profile space to find an empirical equilibrium, it holds one player's strategy fixed at the known theoretical optimum and measures how far the other player's *observed* strategy falls from it, which is closer to an audit than a full EGTA sweep.

**Multi-agent LLM systems.** Cemri et al. (2025) catalog why multi-agent LLM systems fail, building a taxonomy of failure modes rather than a single aggregate error rate — methodologically the closest relative of this project's deviation taxonomy, which likewise classifies *how* a dialogue deviates (toward fairness, toward exploitation, or into outright breakdown) rather than reporting only a mean distance. Du et al. (2024) show that multi-round debate between LLM instances improves factual accuracy, establishing that repeated LLM-LLM interaction is a meaningful object of study in its own right, which motivates this project's LLM-vs-LLM extension (Section 4.5) as a natural complement to the scripted-opponent main study. Mao et al. (2025) place LLM agents inside a general simulation framework for game-theoretic research (Alympics), covering a broad suite of games; the present project trades that breadth for depth on a single, classical game with a closed-form, uniquely-determined prediction.

**LLM negotiation benchmarks.** A growing line of work evaluates LLMs specifically as negotiators. Kwon et al. (2024) and Bianchi et al. (2024, *NegotiationArena*) evaluate negotiation-dialogue competence and tactical behavior in unstructured, multi-scenario settings without a formal equilibrium ground truth; Xia et al. (2024) and Bergemann, Ghili, Hu, Li, and Yang (2026) study bargaining under *incomplete* information — asymmetric private valuations in a buyer/seller or bilateral-trade setting — and, in the latter case, fine-tune models against a fixed opponent rather than evaluating them zero-shot. Liang and Xu (2026) come closest in spirit to the present design, auditing LLM-vs-LLM supply-chain bargaining against a solved Perfect Bayesian Equilibrium and reporting both agreement delay and surplus division, much as this project reports distance-to-SPE and breakdown rate; their setting is private-information, however, which is exactly what rules out a closed-form, complete-information benchmark of the kind Rubinstein's model supplies. Albayaydh and Zhao (2026) ask a question with nearly the same title as this project's — "do LLM agents negotiate rationally?" — but from a mechanism-design angle, verifying whether agents comply with a structured offer protocol rather than measuring how close their offers land to the game's rational prediction.

**This project's position.** None of the above combines (a) a scripted opponent that plays the *exact* theoretical optimum, giving a noise-free baseline; (b) a single classical model (Rubinstein 1982) instantiated across finite, infinite, and asymmetric-discount variants with closed-form predictions for all three; (c) an explicit manipulation of whether the model is told the equilibrium, isolating knowledge from execution; and (d) a predict-then-play elicitation that asks the model to state its belief before it plays. That combination is this project's contribution: a controlled, complete-information audit of LLM bargaining against a provably rational benchmark, with a taxonomy of *how* deviations happen rather than only how large they are.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 The game

Two players, A and B, bargain over a pie of fixed size (normalized to 100 points). They alternate proposing a division: on each round, the proposer names a share they keep for themselves (the remainder going to the other player), and the responder either accepts — ending the game with that split — or rejects, sending play to the next round under rules that depend on the variant (Section 3.3). If no agreement is ever reached, both players receive a payoff of zero. This is Rubinstein's (1982) alternating-offers bargaining game; the question this project asks is how closely an LLM in the proposer-or-responder seat tracks the game's unique theoretical prediction.

### 3.2 Subgame-perfect equilibrium by backward induction

The game's prediction is derived by solving the *last* possible round first and working backward — the textbook application of subgame-perfect equilibrium (SPE) via backward induction. At the final round, the proposer has no further threat point to bargain against, so the unique equilibrium action is for the proposer to claim the entire remaining pie. One round earlier, the proposer need only offer the responder exactly what the responder would get by rejecting and becoming proposer at that final round, discounted by the cost of the extra round of delay; unwinding this recursion to round one yields a single predicted split for any finite deadline, for the stationary infinite-horizon case, and for the case of two unequally patient players. Three closed forms, used throughout this project to score every dialogue:

- **Finite horizon, common discount factor δ, k rounds remaining:** v₁ = 1 (the proposer in the final round takes everything); vₖ = 1 − δ·vₖ₋₁ for k ≥ 2. The round-one proposer's equilibrium share is v_T for a T-round game.
- **Infinite horizon, symmetric δ:** the stationary first-mover share is 1 / (1 + δ).
- **Asymmetric discount factors (δ_proposer, δ_other):** the first-mover's share is (1 − δ_other) / (1 − δ_proposer·δ_other).

All three follow directly from Rubinstein (1982); the key assumption underlying each is *common knowledge of rationality and of the discount factor(s)* — both players know the deadline (or δ) and know the other side will play optimally. Swapping an LLM in for one of the two idealized players is precisely a test of whether that assumption, and the behavior it implies, holds for a non-human reasoner.

### 3.3 Implementing "infinite horizon" on a computer

A literally infinite game cannot be run. Binmore, Rubinstein, and Wolinsky's (1986) risk-of-breakdown equivalence licenses an exact finite implementation: a constant discount factor δ is strategically identical to a constant per-round probability δ that the negotiation continues (and 1 − δ that it collapses to a (0, 0) payoff). The infinite-horizon condition is therefore implemented as: after every rejected offer, continue to the next round with probability δ, and end in breakdown with probability 1 − δ — bounded by a safety-net round cap sized so that the probability a well-behaved game would still be running past the cap is at most 1% (δ = 0.95 is the one exception, capped at 50 rounds for cost control, which leaves an untruncated tail of roughly 7.7%). The asymmetric-discount-factor condition does not admit the same two-factor risk-of-breakdown equivalence, so it instead uses deterministic continuation up to the same 50-round safety-net cap — a documented simplification relative to the symmetric case.

### 3.4 Experimental design (Phase 1): LLM vs. a scripted rational opponent

The core study pits each LLM against a **scripted opponent** that always proposes and accepts at exactly its SPE value — a deterministic, noise-free rational baseline chosen so that any measured deviation is attributable to the LLM rather than to noise contributed by both sides. The LLM always occupies player A's seat (and so is always the first proposer); the scripted opponent occupies player B. Two **prompt conditions** manipulate whether the system prompt states the game's exact equilibrium share (*explained*) or leaves the model to work it out from the rules alone (*unexplained*), separating a failure to know the rational answer from a failure to act on it once known. The full design crosses:

- **Seven game variants**: three infinite-horizon δ ∈ {0.5, 0.8, 0.95}; three finite-horizon deadlines T ∈ {2, 3, 5} at a shared δ = 0.8; one asymmetric-discount case (δ_A = 0.95, δ_B = 0.5).
- **Three model families**: Claude Sonnet 4.5, GPT-5-mini, Gemini 2.5 Flash.
- **Two prompt conditions**: explained / unexplained.

for 42 cells × N = 30 independent dialogues/cell = 1,260 dialogues, the sample size fixed in advance in response to the professor's feedback on the project proposal.

### 3.5 Phase 2 extensions

Two further designs probe the Phase-1 result from different angles, each run at a reduced N = 10/cell appropriate to an exploratory follow-up rather than a second fully-powered study.

**Predict-then-play.** Before each dialogue (unexplained condition only — the explained condition would make the question circular), the model is asked a side-channel question, outside the negotiation transcript: to state its best estimate of the game-theoretically rational first-mover share for the exact game it is about to play. It then plays the ordinary unexplained-condition dialogue against the scripted opponent. Comparing the *stated prediction* to the *true SPE share* and to the model's *own realized play* directly separates two Phase-1-confounded explanations for the knowing-vs-doing gap: a model that predicts close to the true value but plays far from it knows the equilibrium and fails to act on it; a model that predicts far from the true value does not know it in the first place.

**LLM-vs-LLM.** The scripted opponent is replaced with a second LLM — the same model playing itself (same-family) or a different model (cross-family) — to test whether the Phase-1 result (measured against a deterministic rational opponent) generalizes to a setting where the opponent is itself a reasoning, occasionally irrational agent. Player A is always the first-listed model in a pairing and always proposes first; there is no counterbalancing of which model occupies which seat, a documented scope limitation (Section 5.2) adopted to keep this extension affordable. The design covers six pairings (three same-family: Claude–Claude, GPT-5-mini–GPT-5-mini, Gemini–Gemini; three cross-family: Claude–GPT-5-mini, Claude–Gemini, GPT-5-mini–Gemini) crossed with two representative game variants (infinite-horizon δ = 0.8, and finite-horizon T = 3 at δ = 0.8 — both central points of the Phase-1 grid) and the same two prompt conditions as Phase 1, applied symmetrically to both players.

### 3.6 Metrics

**Distance to SPE** is the primary outcome: |realized first-mover share − theoretical SPE share|, measured in pie points (0–100 scale), computed only over dialogues that reached agreement. **Breakdown rate** is the share of dialogues that end without agreement — hitting a deadline, a stochastic breakdown draw, the round-cap safety net, or an unparseable model response — reported separately rather than folded into the distance metric, since a breakdown and a bad-but-agreed split are different kinds of failure. Both carry 95% confidence intervals: a percentile bootstrap (2,000 resamples) for the distance mean, robust to the right-skew expected at N = 30; a Wilson score interval for the breakdown proportion, robust near 0%. A single disagreement-scoring rule is applied uniformly across all three game conditions: any dialogue without an accepted offer pays (0, 0) and is logged with its specific breakdown reason.

**Deviation taxonomy.** Every *agreed* dialogue is additionally classified into one of four categories, letting the analysis speak to *how* a model deviates rather than only *how much*: **SPE-consistent** (realized share within 2 points of the equilibrium share); **fairness-pull** (realized share lies strictly between the fair 50/50 split and the equilibrium share — pulling toward an even split rather than the cold equilibrium); **first-/second-mover-exploitation** (realized share lies beyond the equilibrium share, in the same direction the equilibrium already favors — i.e., claiming *more* advantage than even full rationality would justify); and **backward-induction-failure** (realized share lies on the *opposite* side of 50/50 from the equilibrium prediction — the sign of the deviation is wrong, not just its magnitude). The T = 2 finite-horizon variant is the one case among the seven where the equilibrium itself favors the *second* mover, so the exploitation label is written as second-mover-exploitation there; the underlying logic (deviating further in the direction the equilibrium already favors) is identical.


## 4. Experimental Setup and Results

### 4.1 Models and infrastructure

All three models were accessed through the TAMU API gateway (OpenAI-compatible interface): `protected.Claude Sonnet 4.5` (temperature 1.0, max response tokens 16,384 — the vendor-documented minimum for Claude's thinking mode), `protected.gpt-5-mini` (temperature 0.7, max response tokens 4,000), and `protected.gemini-2.5-flash` (temperature 0.7, max response tokens 16,384). Offers and decisions were elicited via an explicit end-of-response tag (`OFFER: <number>`, `DECISION: ACCEPT|REJECT`) rather than strict JSON, which tolerated Claude's and Gemini's thinking-mode preambles better than a strict schema would have. Each API call appended a short random nonce to the user message, labeled explicitly as bookkeeping to ignore; this was necessary because the gateway was found empirically to cache responses for byte-identical requests, which would otherwise have collapsed every "independent" trial in a cell — which all start from the same empty history — into a single repeated draw. Phase 1 data collection ran October 20–27, 2026; Phase 2 ran November 18, 2026.

**A methodological finding, reported for reproducibility.** Both `gemini-2.5-flash` (at an original cap of 300 response tokens) and `gpt-5-mini` (at an original cap of 2,000) were found, after Phase 1 data collection, to occasionally exhaust their entire response-token budget on hidden reasoning before ever emitting the required tag — a failure that the harness correctly logged as a parse failure (and hence a breakdown), but one that is not a genuine strategic decision to abandon the negotiation. Checking directly: **100% of Gemini's 142 parse-failure breakdowns and 100% of GPT-5-mini's 30 parse-failure breakdowns in the Phase-1 dataset landed at exactly their model's token cap.** Distance-to-SPE is unaffected (it is computed only over dialogues that reached agreement), but the Phase-1 breakdown rates reported below for these two models should be read as an *upper bound* on genuine strategic breakdown, not a clean behavioral measure, until re-collected under a corrected budget. The caps were raised for Phase 2 (`gemini-2.5-flash` to 16,384 after 1,000 and 3,000 both proved insufficient — Gemini's truncation point tracked whatever cap it was given almost exactly, indicating the entire budget was being consumed every time, not occasionally; `gpt-5-mini` to 4,000, which fully resolved its truncation in a 24-dialogue smoke test). This is flagged here, rather than silently re-reported, because distinguishing a real behavioral finding from a measurement artifact is itself part of this kind of experiment's job.

| Model | Distance to SPE — unexplained | Breakdown % — unexplained | Distance to SPE — explained | Breakdown % — explained |

| Claude Sonnet 4.5 | 9.0 | 7.1% | 0.6 | 0.0% |

The headline pattern is consistent across all three models: telling the model the equilibrium share collapses its distance from it by roughly an order of magnitude or more (Figures 1–2 give the full per-variant breakdown with 95% confidence intervals). Left to compute the equilibrium itself, every model lands meaningfully off it — nine to fourteen points on a hundred-point pie — but once the answer is handed to it in the prompt, each model is very good at acting on it. This is the dominant finding of Phase 1: the gap between an LLM and the rational benchmark is, for the most part, a *knowing* problem rather than a *doing* problem.

Breakdown rates tell a second, model-specific story. Claude's and GPT-5-mini's breakdown rates are low in both conditions and fall further once the equilibrium is explained. Gemini's breakdown rate is far higher in both conditions — over half of its unexplained-condition dialogues and nearly a third of its explained-condition dialogues end without agreement — and, per Section 4.1, some unknown share of this is a token-budget artifact rather than genuine negotiating behavior; Phase 2a's predict-then-play data, collected under the corrected token budget, speaks directly to whether Gemini's elevated breakdown rate survives the fix (Section 4.4).

Table 2 classifies every *agreed* Phase-1 dialogue by the deviation taxonomy of Section 3.6 (pooled across all seven variants), revealing that the three models do not fail the same way.

**Table 2. Deviation taxonomy, unexplained condition, pooled across 7 variants (n = 210/model).**

| Model | SPE-consistent | Fairness-pull | Exploitation | Backward-induction-failure | Breakdown |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Claude Sonnet 4.5 | 42.4% | 41.4% | 7.6% | 1.4% | 7.1% |

Claude's unexplained-condition deviations are dominated by fairness-pull: 41.4% of its negotiations land between the fair 50/50 split and the true equilibrium share, consistent with a model compromising toward a social norm rather than computing the game-theoretic optimum. ### 4.4 Phase 2a: predict-then-play

The predict-then-play extension (Section 3.5) ran to completion: 210/210 dialogues across all 21 unexplained-condition cells (7 variants x 3 models), confirmed free of infrastructure errors. Table 3 compares each model's stated prediction of the equilibrium share — elicited as a side-channel question before any negotiation began — against its own realized play in the dialogue that followed, both measured as distance from the game's true, fixed SPE share.

**Table 3. Predicted vs. played distance to true SPE, Phase 2a (N=10/cell x 7 variants = 70 dialogues/model), mean with 95% bootstrap CI.**

| Model | Stated prediction error | Realized play error (agreed only) | n agreed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Claude Sonnet 4.5 | 14.7 [11.4, 18.2] | 10.0 [6.7, 13.9] | 64/70 |
| GPT-5-mini | 14.2 [11.1, 17.7] | 14.0 [10.7, 17.4] | 69/70 |
| Gemini 2.5 Flash | 17.7 [12.9, 23.0] | 11.6 [8.5, 15.2] | 62/70 |

This result complicates the simple "knows it but doesn't act on it" reading of the Phase-1 gap. That hypothesis predicts a *small* prediction error paired with a *large* play error; instead, for all three models the point estimate for prediction error is as large as, or larger than, the point estimate for play error, though the confidence intervals overlap enough (particularly for Claude and Gemini) that this should not be read as a statistically decisive reversal. The more defensible reading is that these models do not have a clean, reliably-elicitable internal representation of the game's equilibrium that is simply being withheld during play: asking directly for the rational answer, stripped of the turn-by-turn structure of an actual negotiation, does not produce an answer any closer to correct than what the model's own offers reveal once it is actually playing — and for Claude and Gemini, played behavior tracks the true equilibrium *more* closely than the model's own stated prediction. One structural contributor, checked directly: for Claude and Gemini, dialogues that reach agreement in a *later* round (where the scripted opponent has had a chance to counter-propose at its own exact SPE value, which the LLM then accepts) land closer to the true SPE (7.3 and 9.0 points of error, respectively) than round-one agreements, where the realized split is entirely the LLM's own first offer (10.7 and 12.8 points of error) — some of play's apparent advantage over prediction is the scripted opponent's own correctness bleeding into the outcome whenever a dialogue runs past round one, not purely the LLM's own accuracy. GPT-5-mini shows the opposite pattern (later-round agreements are *less* accurate, 19.3 vs. 11.1 points), so this correction effect is not universal.

A second finding from this extension, independent of the prediction-vs-play comparison: breakdown rates collected here — under the token-budget fix described in Section 4.1 — are dramatically lower for Gemini than in Phase 1's unexplained condition (11.4% [6%, 21%] here vs. 51.9% in Phase 1) and substantially lower for GPT-5-mini as well (1.4% [0%, 8%] here vs. 12.9% in Phase 1); Claude, whose token cap was unaffected by the bug, shows a similar rate in both (8.6% here vs. 7.1% in Phase 1). Figure 4 presents both comparisons side by side. This is not a controlled apples-to-apples replication — Phase 2a uses N=10/cell rather than Phase 1's N=30, a different random seed, and an added side-channel API call per dialogue — but the direction and magnitude of the shift for exactly the two models whose caps were raised, and the absence of a shift for the one model whose cap was unchanged, is close to the cleanest available confirmation that the Phase-1 token-truncation artifact was a real and substantial contributor to the originally reported breakdown rates, not a false alarm.

### 4.5 Phase 2b: LLM-vs-LLM

The LLM-vs-LLM extension (Section 3.5) also ran to completion: 240/240 dialogues across all 24 cells (2 variants x 6 pairings x 2 conditions), confirmed free of infrastructure errors. Table 4 reports distance-to-SPE and breakdown rate by pairing type (same-family: a model against itself; cross-family: two different models) and prompt condition, pooled across the two variants tested.

**Table 4. Distance to SPE and breakdown rate, Phase 2b, by pairing type and condition (n = 60/cell).**

| Pairing type | Condition | Distance to SPE | Breakdown % |
|---|---|---|---|
| Same-family | Unexplained | 17.7 [12.0, 24.4] | 25.0% [16, 37] |
| Same-family | Explained | 0.3 [0.1, 0.7] | 6.7% [3, 16] |
| Cross-family | Unexplained | 12.5 [11.0, 13.9] | 26.7% [17, 39] |
| Cross-family | Explained | 2.2 [1.1, 3.5] | 13.3% [7, 24] |

Two patterns stand out (Figure 5). First, **breakdown rates against another LLM are substantially higher than Phase 1's breakdown rates against the scripted rational opponent** — roughly 25-27% unexplained here, versus 7-52% across the three models in Phase 1 (where only Claude's and GPT-5-mini's numbers are directly comparable, since Gemini's Phase-1 figure is inflated by the token-truncation artifact). This is consistent with a simple mechanical explanation: a scripted opponent that always proposes and accepts at its exact SPE value effectively "catches" an LLM's imperfect offer whenever the LLM's proposal clears the opponent's threshold, and never itself contributes a mistake; two LLMs negotiating with each other have no such guaranteed-rational anchor, so errors from *either* side can produce a breakdown, and the two can share correlated blind spots rather than one canceling the other's noise. Second, the pattern **flips between prompt conditions**: unexplained, same-family pairings are *worse* than cross-family (17.7 vs. 12.5 distance), but explained, same-family pairings are *better* than cross-family (0.3 vs. 2.2 distance, with cross-family's confidence interval not including zero). A plausible reading is that two instances of the same model share the same failure mode when reasoning is required (amplifying a shared blind spot when neither side is told the answer) but converge cleanly on the same stated equilibrium once it is simply handed to both of them, whereas two different models can retain small disagreements about the intended split even when both have been told the same number.

At the level of individual pairings (not shown in the pooled table), two cells are worth flagging by name for how extreme they are. **Gemini-vs-Gemini, unexplained** is the single worst cell collected anywhere in this project: 29.5 points of distance and a 40% breakdown rate, well beyond Gemini's own (already elevated, and partly artifactual) Phase-1 figure against the scripted opponent. **GPT-5-mini-vs-Gemini, unexplained** breaks down in fully half of its dialogues (50%) — a rate neither model reaches on its own against a rational counterpart, and a clear example of an emergent pairing-specific failure that a single-model evaluation (Phase 1) or even a same-family evaluation could not have surfaced. In both cases, stating the equilibrium in the prompt resolves the problem almost completely: Gemini-vs-Gemini explained falls to 0.59 distance and 0% breakdown, and GPT-5-mini-vs-Gemini explained falls to 0.56 distance and 0% breakdown — the knowing-vs-doing pattern from Phase 1 (Section 4.2) replicates even in these worst-case pairings. Claude's pairings (with itself, GPT-5-mini, or Gemini) are comparatively stable across both conditions, suggesting Claude plays a moderating role in a mixed pairing regardless of which model it faces.

## 5. Discussion

### 5.1 Interpreting the Phase 1 result: knowing vs. doing

The roughly order-of-magnitude collapse in distance-to-SPE once the equilibrium is stated in the prompt, replicated across all three model families, is the clearest signal in this study. It argues against a simple "LLMs cannot do game theory" reading of the unexplained-condition results: the mechanism separating LLMs from the rational benchmark is predominantly a failure to surface the correct backward-induction answer under their own reasoning, not an inability to execute on it once it is given. This distinction matters practically: a deployed negotiation agent that is told, or can be made to compute, the relevant equilibrium share is a very different risk profile from one that cannot act on it even when told. Section 4.4's predict-then-play results bear directly on how much of the "not knowing" is a genuine computation failure versus a model that privately computes something close to the right answer but does not use it strategically.

### 5.2 Model-specific failure signatures

The deviation taxonomy (Section 4.3) shows that "distance from equilibrium" is not one failure mode but at least three, distributed differently across models. Claude's unexplained-condition deviations lean toward fairness-pull, compromising toward an even split, which reads as a norm-following tendency surviving into a context where the norm is not actually optimal for the player with equilibrium leverage. GPT-5-mini's deviations lean the opposite way, toward exploitation beyond what even the equilibrium would justify, a more aggressive and arguably more concerning pattern for a deployed negotiation agent, since it risks provoking breakdown against a less patient real counterpart than the scripted opponent used here. Gemini's story is dominated by its breakdown rate rather than by the shape of its agreed splits, and per Section 4.1 at least part of that elevated rate is a measurement artifact rather than a behavioral one; whether a genuine breakdown-proneness survives the token-budget fix is addressed directly by the LLM-vs-LLM data (Section 4.5), collected under the corrected setting.

### 5.3 What Phase 2 adds

The two Phase 2 extensions sharpen and partially complicate the Phase-1 knowing-vs-doing story rather than simply confirming it. Predict-then-play shows that the "knowing" side of the story is weaker than Phase 1 alone suggests: when asked directly to state the equilibrium, before any negotiation context, models do not produce an answer reliably closer to correct than what their own in-context play reveals (Section 4.4) — the gap between an LLM and the rational benchmark looks less like a correct answer being computed and withheld, and more like the models lacking a stable, portable representation of the equilibrium that holds up outside the turn-by-turn structure of actually playing. At the same time, Phase 2a's cleanest result is unambiguous support for a *different* part of the Phase-1 story: the token-budget methodological finding (Section 4.1) is directly confirmed by a large, model-specific drop in breakdown rate exactly where the fix was applied.

LLM-vs-LLM extends the central "explaining the equilibrium closes the gap" finding to a genuinely harder setting: it holds even when *both* players are LLMs, including in the two most extreme failure cases observed anywhere in this project (Gemini-vs-Gemini and GPT-5-mini-vs-Gemini, both unexplained), where simply stating the equilibrium to both sides collapses a near-total failure into near-equilibrium play. But it also reveals something Phase 1's scripted-opponent design could not: real, substantial risk that only appears when both negotiating parties are imperfect reasoners rather than one perfect and one imperfect. A deployed negotiation agent audited only against a rational baseline, as in Phase 1, would look considerably safer than the same agent actually is once it negotiates against another LLM of uncertain reliability — the GPT-5-mini-vs-Gemini unexplained cell's 50% breakdown rate has no analogue in either model's individual Phase-1 numbers.

### 5.4 Limitations

Several limitations bound how far these results generalize. First, the Phase-1 main result is measured against a scripted, deterministic rational opponent; it establishes how an LLM behaves when the other side is guaranteed not to deviate, which is a cleaner but less realistic setting than bargaining against another reasoning agent. Phase 2's LLM-vs-LLM extension speaks to this but at a much smaller sample size (N = 10/cell versus Phase 1's pre-registered N = 30/cell) and over only two of the seven game variants, so it should be read as a targeted probe rather than a second fully-powered study. Second, for cost control the three finite-horizon deadlines share a single discount factor (delta = 0.8) rather than crossing every deadline with every discount factor, so the finite-horizon results characterize behavior at one point in a larger (T, delta) space rather than the whole space. Third, the asymmetric-discount-factor condition uses deterministic continuation rather than the stochastic risk-of-breakdown equivalence used for the symmetric infinite-horizon condition, since that equivalence has no clean two-discount-factor generalization, a documented simplification rather than an oversight. Fourth, as detailed in Section 4.1, part of Gemini's and GPT-5-mini's Phase-1 breakdown rates reflects a token-budget measurement artifact rather than pure strategic behavior; the artifact does not affect the distance-to-SPE numbers, which are computed only over agreed dialogues, but it does mean the Phase-1 breakdown-rate comparison across these two models should be read as an upper bound. Fifth, neither the distance-to-SPE computation nor the deviation-taxonomy classification was spot-checked by hand against a sample of raw transcripts; both are produced by an automated parsing and scoring pipeline, and while the parser's offline unit tests pass and the live data showed no anomalous values on inspection, no manual audit of model reasoning text was performed.

### 5.5 Alternative explanations considered

An alternative reading of the unexplained-condition fairness-pull pattern (Claude in particular; Section 4.3) is that it reflects genuine strategic caution rather than a norm-following bias: a model unsure of its own backward-induction calculation might rationally hedge toward a safer, more acceptable-looking offer rather than risk a confidently wrong extreme claim. The predict-then-play data (Section 4.4) weighs against the "deliberate compromise" half of this alternative: if Claude's played fairness-pull reflected a correct private calculation being deliberately softened, its *stated* prediction of the equilibrium should land close to the true value, since nothing about the prediction question discourages a confident answer. Instead, Claude's mean prediction error (14.7 points) is numerically larger than its mean play error (10.0 points) — the model is not demonstrably sitting on a correct answer. The more consistent reading is that the fairness-pull is closer to an honest best guess under genuine uncertainty than a knowing compromise, though the overlapping confidence intervals mean this is suggestive rather than conclusive.

A second alternative worth naming: Gemini's dramatically elevated breakdown rate in LLM-vs-LLM pairings (Section 4.5) could reflect either genuine strategic incompatibility with certain counterparts, or simply a higher residual rate of response-formatting failures that the Section 4.1 fix reduced but did not eliminate. Checking directly for the most extreme cell: of the GPT-5-mini-vs-Gemini unexplained dialogues that broke down (10 of 20), only 3 were parse failures; the other 7 were genuine game-mechanical outcomes — 5 hit the infinite-horizon condition's built-in stochastic-breakdown draw and 2 ran out the finite-horizon deadline. That rules out "mostly a formatting artifact" as the explanation for this particular cell: the two models were genuinely failing to converge on a split fast enough to beat the game's own clock, more often than either model does against the scripted rational opponent, which is a real coordination failure rather than a measurement artifact.

## 6. Conclusion

Returning to the research question: no, LLMs do not spontaneously play the Rubinstein subgame-perfect equilibrium. Left to compute it themselves against a perfectly rational opponent, all three models tested deviate by a meaningful nine to fourteen points on a hundred-point pie, and the three models deviate in qualitatively different ways — Claude toward fairness, GPT-5-mini toward exploitation, Gemini toward outright breakdown. But the dominant mechanism behind that gap is closer to a failure to *know* the equilibrium than a failure to *act* on it: simply stating the equilibrium share in the prompt closes most of the gap, for every model tested, in every setting examined — against a scripted rational opponent (Phase 1), and even against another LLM in the two most extreme failure pairings observed in this project (Phase 2b). The predict-then-play extension complicates the "knowing" half of that story, though: asked to state the equilibrium directly, before playing, none of the three models produces an answer reliably closer to the truth than what its own in-context play already reveals, suggesting the gap is better described as the models lacking a stable, portable representation of the equilibrium rather than withholding a correct one. And the LLM-vs-LLM extension shows that auditing against a rational baseline alone, as Phase 1 does, understates a real risk: two imperfect models negotiating with each other can fail to converge far more often than either does against a guaranteed-rational counterpart, with no analogue in the single-model Phase-1 results.

One honest methodological lesson sits alongside the substantive ones: a measurement artifact — a response-token cap too tight for two of three models under test — was silently inflating breakdown rates for exactly those two models in the original Phase-1 collection, and was only caught by building a follow-up extension and noticing an implausible pattern in the raw numbers. The fix is documented, and Phase 2a's dramatic before/after breakdown-rate shift for the affected models is itself evidence that the fix mattered. Reporting this rather than quietly re-running Phase 1 under the corrected setting was a deliberate choice: distinguishing a genuine finding from a measurement artifact, and being transparent about which is which, is as much a part of this kind of empirical study as the headline numbers are.

Future work beyond this project's scope includes a full T x delta factorial for the finite-horizon condition now that the API budget picture is better understood; a human-baseline comparison to calibrate how far "nine to fourteen points off equilibrium" actually is relative to human bargainers, who are themselves well documented not to play the SPE exactly; a full-scale (N = 30/cell) replication of the LLM-vs-LLM and predict-then-play extensions, which here ran at a smaller, exploratory N; a manual transcript audit to separate genuine strategic breakdown from residual formatting failure in the highest-breakdown cells; and few-shot or fine-tuning interventions that teach the equilibrium concept generally, rather than simply stating the answer for one game instance at a time.

**Code and data.** The full harness and raw results are published at **https://github.com/rahmarx/rubinstein-llm-bargaining** (public repository; commit `6095745` is the version used for every table and figure in this report). It includes: the game engine (`bargaining_env.py`), scripted SPE opponent (`scripted_agent.py`), LLM agent wrapper (`llm_agent.py`), three experiment orchestrators (`run_experiments.py` for Phase 1, `predict_then_play.py` and `llm_vs_llm.py` for Phase 2), statistical analysis (`analysis.py`, `deviation_taxonomy.py`), plotting (`plot_results.py`, `plot_predict_then_play.py`, `plot_llm_vs_llm.py`), the raw result files (`data/phase1_results.json`, `data/phase2_predict_then_play.json`, `data/phase2_llm_vs_llm.json`), and the figures used in this report (`report/figures/`). The repository's README documents setup (`pip install -r src/requirements.txt`, credentials in `.env`), the exact commands to reproduce each phase (e.g. `python run_experiments.py --n 30 --budget 8.0`), and every bug found and fixed during the project, including the token-truncation issue in Section 4.1. Offline unit/sanity checks requiring no API credentials are built into each module (`python bargaining_env.py`, `python scripted_agent.py`, `python llm_agent.py`).

**Models and access.** `protected.Claude Sonnet 4.5`, `protected.gpt-5-mini`, `protected.gemini-2.5-flash`, accessed via the TAMU API gateway's OpenAI-compatible interface between October 20 and November 18, 2026. Exact sampling settings and response-token caps are given in Section 4.1; the token-cap values used for Phase 1 and for Phase 2 differ (as documented) and both are recorded in the code's version history.

**Trial counts, seeds, and failure handling.** Phase 1: N = 30 dialogues/cell, 42 cells, 1,260 total, seed 0, resumable and budget-capped (`--budget` flag enforces a hard USD stop using per-model token costs). Phase 2a (predict-then-play): N = 10 dialogues/cell, 21 cells, seed 1. Phase 2b (LLM-vs-LLM): N = 10 dialogues/cell, 24 cells, seed 2. An unparseable model offer ends a dialogue immediately as a `parse_failure` breakdown; an unparseable accept/reject decision is treated as an implicit reject (logged via a `parse_failed` flag) rather than ending the dialogue, since rejecting is always a legal response and this avoids conflating "the model's text didn't match the expected format" with "the model chose to walk away." Infrastructure-level failures (the shared account's daily budget cap, and the token-truncation artifact described in Section 4.1) are detected programmatically (`analysis.is_infra_error`) and excluded from every statistic rather than counted as breakdowns; the Phase-1 dataset reported here is confirmed to contain zero such exclusions after cleanup.

**Compute/API budget.** Phase 1 (full N=30 run plus pilot and validation calls): approximately $4 total. Phase 2a and 2b were each run with a $3–5 hard budget cap; actual spend is recorded in each run's console log, retained alongside the code.

**Reproducibility limits.** All three models are externally hosted and stochastic (temperature > 0 for two of the three); re-running the exact code will reproduce the *experimental design* exactly but not the *exact numeric results*, since model outputs vary run to run and hosted models may be updated by their providers over time without a pinned snapshot. The scripted-opponent and statistical-analysis code is fully deterministic and reproducible given a fixed set of raw dialogue records.
